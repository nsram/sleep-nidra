A personal project

Sleep Nidra

A guided audio for deep relaxation and sleep — inspired by Yoga Nidra, built for falling asleep.

What this is

Each track is about 24 minutes of spoken guidance, framed by chanting, designed to be played in bed at night. It borrows the architecture of traditional Yoga Nidra — settling the body, setting an intention (sankalpa), a slow rotation of awareness through the body, breath awareness and counting, and a short visualization — but every choice in it serves one purpose: letting you fall asleep.

A clarification: This is not a classical Yoga Nidra session and doesn't claim to be one. It's a sleep aid built on Yoga Nidra's principles, alongside the author's experience with Transcendental Meditation, Sudarshan Kriya, and Vipassana. If you're looking for traditional instruction, seek a qualified teacher in the Bihar School lineage.

Listen

Best in bed, lights out, low volume. Unlike classic yoga nidra, there is deliberately no wake-up ending — the track dissolves into silence and a soft closing chant.

Transcrição

On Om and Om Shanti

Every track opens with three chanted Oms and closes with a soft Om Shanti — both chanted by Sriram himself, recorded at home, not synthesized.

In the yogic tradition, Om is regarded as the primordial sound — the vibration underlying everything. Here the three opening Oms mark a threshold: the day is set aside, the practice begins. They are given at full voice, the way you'd chant them sitting up before lying down.

Om Shanti means "Om, peace" — shanti is peace. It closes the track the way a candle is snuffed rather than blown out: a gentle seal, sixty seconds of silence after the last spoken words, then the soft chant. There is deliberately no instruction to wake up. The track is meant to hand you over to sleep.

How these were made

  1. Script, iterated by ear. The English script went through eight versions, revised after full listen-throughs in bed. The guiding rule: sparse words, long silences — the pauses are the nidra. The final script is 85 short cues separated by 8–160 seconds of silence.
  2. Voice. The guidance is AI text-to-speech: a warm male voice for English, and native-accented voices for Spanish, French, German, and Brazilian Portuguese. (An American-accented voice "speaking Spanish" was tried and rejected — it sounded like two different people.)
  3. Assembly, cue by cue. Each cue was synthesized separately and laid onto the exact silence structure — not narrated straight through. The long countdown silence (~3 minutes of undisturbed breath-counting) is the heart of the track.
  4. Chants. Sriram recorded the opening three Oms and the closing Om Shanti on his phone, then trimmed and lightly enriched them (warmth, a touch of hall reverb). The opening plays at full level; the closing is kept soft.
  5. Mastering. Peak level −1.3 dB, no clipping. After the final words, 60 seconds of silence precede the closing chant.
  6. Translation. The non-English scripts were translated from the final English and are currently awaiting review by native speakers — which is exactly what the feedback buttons are for.

Inspiration

The starting point was the 45-minute Yoga Nidra of the Bihar School of Yoga tradition (Swami Niranjanananda Saraswati) — a dense, classical session that Sriram used for a few years. The YouTube link has since been taken down. He came to the practice after learning that India's Prime Minister, Narendra Modi, uses Yoga Nidra as part of his own routine.

The classical session was too dense and too long for assisting sleep onset — so it was rebuilt to be shorter, sparser, warmer, ending in silence instead of a wake-up directive. Along the way it absorbed lessons from years of Transcendental Meditation, Sudarshan Kriya, and Vipassana practice: the value of a simple repeated anchor, of the breath as a vehicle, and of doing less.

For the authentic tradition, the Bihar School publishes its own recordings officially — originally designed Yoga Nidra sessions by Swami Satyananda Saraswati in many languages — at biharyoga.net and through the Satyam Yoga Prasad app and website.

Feedback

These are works in progress — especially the translations. If you listen, I'd genuinely like to know:

  • Does the voice sound natural and soothing, or is anything off?
  • Is the pacing right — or do pauses feel too long, too short?
  • For non-English versions: is the translation natural? Anything awkward or wrong?
  • Did you fall asleep? (The highest praise available.)
